## Use for planning conversations

Use the document's general planning ideas when a student needs a course and application roadmap:

- Work backward from the intended medical-school entry year to the application cycle, exam preparation, and courses.
- Start long prerequisite sequences early. Avoid postponing all demanding science classes until the end of college.
- Place demanding courses first, then balance the rest of the semester with major requirements, electives, religion, work, and experiences.
- Treat the suggested two demanding courses, one moderate course, one lighter course, and one religion course as a starting pattern, not a required schedule. This document suggests 14–15 credits; the separate course cheat sheet suggests 14–16.
- Begin sustainable clinical exposure, service, shadowing, leadership, and research exploration early enough to avoid a last-minute workload.
- Encourage sustained involvement, reflection, and relationships. Ask what the student learned about serving people and pursuing medicine.
- Consider flex-semester coursework when it fits the student's track, course availability, finances, and need for a break.

## Verify before using specific claims

The source lists PHYS 105/106, CHEM 105/106/351/352/481 or a biology-department alternative, BIO 180/181, recommended BIO 461, psychology, and sociology. Treat these as source-listed planning leads. Check the current BYU–Idaho catalog, prerequisites, labs, offerings, and exam content before recommending a sequence. Distinguish exam preparation from degree and professional-school prerequisites. Do not apply this MCAT list to the DAT.

The source also includes fixed experience-hour targets, a fixed April exam recommendation, a June 20 “absolute latest” exam statement, a minimum four-semester chemistry claim, and broad claims about research, publications, and medical-school workload. **These are not validated universal requirements.** Do not turn them into deadlines, minimums, or guarantees. Resolve the student's actual cycle and school requirements with current official sources.

Use current official requirements for compliance and the student's circumstances for fit. Do not treat a shorter possible course sequence as automatically preferable to a more manageable one.
